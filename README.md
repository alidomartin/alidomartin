# CMJ Force-Time Curve — Phase Guide

A visual reference for the key phases of the **Countermovement Jump (CMJ)** force-time curve, including jump and landing mechanics.

---

## Jump Phases

### Quiet Phase

| Description | Force-Time Curve |
|---|---|
| The first phase of the CMJ. The athlete stands still for at least **1 second** before movement begins. During this period **system weight** (body weight in Newtons) is calculated. It is important to cue the athlete to stand still to keep the validity of the weighing period high. | ![Quiet Phase](images/quiet_phase.png) |

---

### Unweighting Phase

| Description | Force-Time Curve |
|---|---|
| The athlete begins the countermovement by **relaxing the agonist muscles**, resulting in combined flexion of the hips and knees. Force drops **below system weight** — the athlete is essentially in free fall. This phase is defined by a **negative velocity** that is continuing to descend (increasing velocity in the negative direction). | ![Unweighting Phase](images/unweighting_phase.png) |

---

### Braking Phase

| Description | Force-Time Curve |
|---|---|
| The athlete decelerates ("brakes") their **center-of-mass (COM)**. Braking begins when COM velocity is still negative but ascending toward **0 m/s**. The athlete continues to apply force to decelerate their mass — visually still moving downwards. This phase commences from **peak negative COM velocity** through to when COM velocity increases to zero. | ![Braking Phase](images/braking_phase.png) |

---

### Transfer Point

| Description | Force-Time Curve |
|---|---|
| Also called the **switch phase**, this occurs between the braking and propulsive phases. It can be thought of as the **amortization period** — the brief isometric portion when velocity is zero. This is the lowest portion of the CMJ (minimum displacement). An athlete who spends **less time at the bottom** of the "V" is more efficient at transferring momentum. | ![Transfer Point](images/transfer_point.png) |

---

### Propulsive Phase

| Description | Force-Time Curve |
|---|---|
| The athlete forcefully extends hips, knees, and ankles to **propel their COM vertically**. This phase begins when a **positive COM velocity** is achieved (threshold: 0.01 m/s). Key metrics include average/peak relative propulsive force and average/peak propulsive power **(PRPP)**. If an athlete struggles here, Cleans, Trap Bar Jumps, or Pin Squats may help. | ![Propulsive Phase](images/propulsive_phase.png) |

---

### Flight Phase

| Description | Force-Time Curve |
|---|---|
| The athlete leaves the force plates from **take-off** until **touchdown**. Flight time is the outcome of every muscle action and generation of momentum. Note: **Jump Height is calculated using takeoff velocity** (industry gold standard), not flight time. | ![Flight Phase](images/flight_phase.png) |

---

### Landing Phase

| Description | Force-Time Curve |
|---|---|
| The final CMJ phase. Begins when the athlete **contacts the force plate** after the flight phase. The athlete applies a net impulse equal to the propulsion impulse to decelerate COM from contact velocity through to zero. Bilateral force plates can show discrepancies between limbs — key for **return-to-play** and **injury risk** assessment. | ![Landing Phase](images/landing_phase.png) |

---

## Landing Phases — Detail

The CMJ landing curve is subdivided into three distinct phases based on COM velocity and GRF landmarks.

### Full Phase Summary

![CMJ Landing Full Phase Summary](images/landing_summary.png)

---

### Phase 1: Loading

| Description | Force-Time Curve |
|---|---|
| From **initial contact** to **peak GRF**. The loading rate and peak force reflect how rapidly and forcefully the athlete accepts ground contact. A higher loading rate can increase injury risk. Key metrics: **Peak GRF**, **Loading Rate**, **v at contact**. | ![Phase 1 Loading](images/loading_phase.png) |

---

### Phase 2: Attenuation

| Description | Force-Time Curve |
|---|---|
| From **peak GRF** to the **local minimum**. The athlete is absorbing and attenuating the impact force. The force attenuation rate and average force during this window indicate how efficiently the athlete disperses impact energy through eccentric muscle action. | ![Phase 2 Attenuation](images/attenuation_phase.png) |

---

### Phase 3: Control

| Description | Force-Time Curve |
|---|---|
| From the **local minimum** to when **COM velocity = 0** (COM stops). This brief phase reflects the athlete's ability to stabilize after the main impact is absorbed. Key metrics: **Control Time**, **mRSI**, **LPI**. Shorter control time = greater efficiency. | ![Phase 3 Control](images/control_phase.png) |

## Dynamic Rebound Index (DRI)

Apply the brakes on the **Dynamic Rebound Index (DRI)** for interpreting rebound performance.

Accounting for drop height and a challenge to **Reactive Strength Index (RSI)**, the proposed formula for DRI is:

> **DRI = (Drop Height + Rebound Jump Height) / (9.81 × Contact Time²)**

As with any ratio metric, understanding the component parts and how they contribute to a movement outcome is the critical consideration that often gets overlooked when trying to quantify performance in one metric.

You can apply the same logic when interpreting **countermovement rebound jump** performance (CMJ + Rebound Jump) if you're not doing drop jumps in your practice.

---

### Component Metrics

| Component | Units | Role in Formula | What It Reflects |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Drop Height** | m | Numerator (input energy) | Potential energy supplied to the system; sets the eccentric demand |
| **Rebound Jump Height** | m | Numerator (output) | Vertical COM displacement after ground contact; reflects reactive propulsive capability |
| **Contact Time** | s (squared) | Denominator | Time on the ground between landing and take-off; proxy for stiffness and SSC utilisation |
| **9.81** | m·s⁻² | Denominator constant | Gravitational acceleration; converts the CT² term into units that non-dimensionalise the ratio |

---

### Testing Protocols

**Drop Jump (DJ) — DRI primary use case**
1. Athlete stands on a box of known height (typical 20–60 cm).
2. Step off (do **not** jump up) and drop to the force plates.
3. On contact, minimise ground contact time and maximise rebound jump height.
4. Hands on hips throughout to remove arm swing contribution.
5. Record **Drop Height**, **Rebound Jump Height** (from take-off velocity), and **Contact Time**.

**CMJ + Rebound — when drop jumps aren't programmed**
1. Athlete performs a CMJ from the force plates.
2. On landing, immediately rebound into a maximal second jump.
3. Use **CMJ Height** in place of Drop Height in the numerator.
4. Record **CMJ Height**, **Rebound Jump Height**, and **Rebound Contact Time**.

---

### Example Data

| Metric | Athlete A | Athlete B |
|---|---|---|
| CMJ Height | 0.40 m | 0.30 m |
| Rebound Jump Height | 0.35 m | 0.28 m |
| Contact Time | 0.26 s | 0.20 s |
| **DRI** | **1.13** | **1.48** |

Worked calculations:
- Athlete A: (0.40 + 0.35) / (9.81 × 0.26²) = 0.75 / 0.663 ≈ **1.13**
- Athlete B: (0.30 + 0.28) / (9.81 × 0.20²) = 0.58 / 0.392 ≈ **1.48**

Despite Athlete A displacing their centre of mass significantly higher in both **ballistic** and **reactive** tasks, Athlete B attains a higher Dynamic Rebound Index.

![DRI Athlete Comparison](images/dri_athlete_comparison.png)

This occurs because DRI is **dominated by rebound contact time**, which is squared in the denominator of the equation. As a result, relatively small differences in contact time exert a much larger influence on the final score than meaningful differences in jump height.

---

### Contact Time Sensitivity

Holding Drop Height + Rebound Height fixed at 0.75 m, shaving 60 ms off contact time (0.26 s → 0.20 s) inflates DRI by ~69 %. A 10 cm change in combined jump height only shifts DRI by ~13 % at the same contact time.

![DRI Sensitivity to Contact Time](images/dri_sensitivity.png)

**Implication:** ranking athletes by DRI without inspecting the inputs can reward **short ground contacts produced with minimal effort** over **higher rebounds executed with slightly longer contacts** — two very different movement solutions.

In isolation, DRI does not inform **how** the trial was performed, nor does it clearly guide post-test training decisions.

---

### DRI vs RSI — What's Different

| Aspect | RSI (Reactive Strength Index) | DRI (Dynamic Rebound Index) |
|---|---|---|
| Formula | Jump Height / Contact Time | (Drop Height + Rebound Height) / (9.81 × Contact Time²) |
| Units | m·s⁻¹ (dimensional) | Dimensionless ratio |
| Input accounting | Ignores drop height | Includes drop height in numerator |
| CT weighting | Linear | Quadratic (amplifies CT differences) |
| Movement strategy bias | Slight bias toward short CT | Strong bias toward short CT |

DRI attempts to correct for the missing drop-height input in RSI, but the **CT² denominator** introduces a new over-sensitivity problem.

---

### Interpretation Framework

Use DRI (or RSI) as a **screen**, not a verdict. Before prescribing, always inspect the component parts:

1. **Is rebound height competitive with or higher than drop/CMJ height?** If not, the athlete is losing energy through contact — trainable via plyometric capacity and stiffness work.
2. **Is contact time < 0.25 s for a reactive task?** If not, the intent or SSC strategy may be ballistic rather than reactive — re-brief the cue and retest.
3. **Did the athlete sit into the contact (long CT, low height)?** Suggests poor tendon stiffness or descending strategy — ankle stiffness, pogo, and short-contact drills.
4. **Did the athlete bounce off quickly but without height (short CT, low height)?** Suggests avoidance of load — progressive drop-height loading and bounding.
5. **Compare trial-to-trial within athlete** rather than athlete-to-athlete for training decisions — individual mechanics matter more than cross-sectional rank.

---

### Post-Test Action Guide

| Pattern | Likely Limiter | Training Emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| High CT, low rebound height | Stiffness / reactive capacity | Pogo, ankle stiffness, short-contact hops |
| Short CT, low rebound height | Load tolerance / impulse | Incremental drop height, depth jumps, bounding |
| Long CT, high rebound height | Reactive intent / cueing | Cue "floor is hot", audio target contact time |
| Short CT, high rebound height | At or near expression ceiling | Maintain; vary drop height for dose-response |

---

### Interpretation Note

This aligns with Jason Pedley's views on RSI — with extra ingredients to the recipe:

> *"If we bake a cake, but it doesn't taste right, we need to know the recipe to see what could or should be changed."*

Always interrogate the component parts (drop height, rebound jump height, contact time) alongside the composite score before making training decisions.
